Ignoring Search Intent

🚨 The Mistake: Creating content without understanding what users actually want

🤔 What Is Search Intent?

Search intent (also called user intent) is the reason behind a search query. When someone types a phrase into Google, they have a specific goal in mind — they want to learn something, find a specific website, compare products, or make a purchase.

Search intent is arguably the most important ranking factor in 2026. Google's primary mission is to deliver the most relevant results for each search query. If your content doesn't match what users are actually looking for, you won't rank — no matter how well‑optimised your page is.

The problem? Most website owners create content based on what they think users are searching for, rather than what users are actually typing into Google. This mismatch between intent and content is one of the most common and costly SEO mistakes.

📊 The 4 Types of Search Intent (With Examples)

Understanding the four types of search intent is the first step to fixing this mistake. Here's what each one looks like:

📚

Informational Intent

Goal: Learn something, find an answer, or gain knowledge.

Example searches:

  • "How to start a business in Kenya"
  • "What is SEO?"
  • "Benefits of local SEO for small businesses"
  • "Best accounting software for SMEs Kenya"
What to create: Guides, tutorials, explainer articles, FAQs
🛍️

Commercial Intent

Goal: Research products/services before making a purchase decision.

Example searches:

  • "Best SEO agencies in Nairobi"
  • "Toyota Land Cruiser vs Mitsubishi Pajero"
  • "Top web design companies Kenya 2026"
  • "Shopify vs WooCommerce for Kenyan stores"
What to create: Comparisons, reviews, "best of" lists, case studies
🛒

Transactional Intent

Goal: Make a purchase, sign up, or complete an action.

Example searches:

  • "Buy SEO services Kenya"
  • "Order coffee beans Nairobi"
  • "Book hotel in Diani Beach"
  • "Sign up for email marketing software"
What to create: Product pages, service pages, booking forms, checkout pages
🗺️

Navigational Intent

Goal: Find a specific website or brand.

Example searches:

  • "DK Digital SEO"
  • "Safaricom customer care"
  • "KCB Bank Kenya login"
  • "Jumia Kenya official website"
What to create: Brand pages, homepage, contact page, about page

❌ The Problem: Content That Doesn't Match Intent

Here's a real-world example of what happens when you ignore search intent:

Scenario: You sell accounting services in Nairobi

You create a page targeting the keyword "best accounting software" because it has high search volume. But your page is about your accounting services — not software reviews.

❌ The Problem:

Users searching for "best accounting software" want to compare software options. They are not looking for accounting services. When they land on your service page, they bounce immediately.

✅ What You Should Have Created:

A page titled "Best Accounting Software for Kenyan Businesses" that reviews and compares the top software options. This would attract the commercial intent audience. You can then include a call-to-action at the end: "Need help implementing these tools? Contact our accounting team."

This is a classic intent mismatch. The content doesn't match what the user actually wants, so they bounce — and Google's algorithms notice.

The result:

  • High bounce rate (users leave immediately)
  • Low time on page
  • Poor engagement metrics
  • Lower rankings over time
  • Wasted traffic that doesn't convert

📉 The Impact: How Intent Mismatch Hurts Your Business

When your content doesn't match search intent, the damage goes beyond just poor rankings:

  • You attract the wrong audience. Users who land on your page are not interested in what you're offering. They leave without converting.
  • Your conversion rate plummets. Even if you get traffic, it doesn't turn into leads or sales because the visitors weren't looking for your product or service.
  • You waste your content budget. You spend time and money creating content that doesn't serve your business goals.
  • Your brand reputation suffers. Users who land on irrelevant pages may perceive your brand as unhelpful or unprofessional.
  • Google demotes your page. Poor user signals (bounce rate, time on page, pogo-sticking) tell Google that your page isn't satisfying users, so they drop your rankings.

📊 The Data:

60% of websites create content without considering search intent. This means that aligning your content with intent instantly gives you a competitive advantage over 60% of your competitors.

🔧 How to Fix It: A Step‑by‑Step Guide

Fixing search intent mismatch is straightforward when you know what to look for. Follow these steps to ensure your content matches what users actually want:

Step 1: Analyse the Search Engine Results Page (SERP)

Before creating any content, search for your target keyword in Google and analyse what's already ranking on page 1. This is the fastest way to understand search intent.

  • Look at the top 10 results. What type of content are they? Are they blog posts? Product pages? Comparison guides? Landing pages?
  • Check the format. Are they listicles? Step‑by‑step guides? In‑depth articles? Short summaries?
  • Look at the "People Also Ask" section. This shows you the most common questions users have about the topic.
  • Examine the featured snippet. What content is Google pulling into the "position zero" answer box? This tells you exactly what Google considers the most helpful content for that query.

Example: If you're targeting "best coffee shops in Nairobi" and the top results are list articles with 10+ recommendations, you should create a list article. Creating a single product page for your coffee shop would not match the intent — users want to see multiple options.

Step 2: Determine the Intent Type

Based on your SERP analysis, determine which of the four intent types the keyword belongs to:

  • Informational: "How to", "What is", "Why does", "Guide to"
  • Commercial: "Best", "Top", "Review", "Comparison", "vs"
  • Transactional: "Buy", "Order", "Book", "Sign up", "Price"
  • Navigational: Brand names, specific website names

Step 3: Match Your Content Format to Intent

Each intent type has a content format that works best:

Intent Type Best Content Format Keywords That Signal Intent
Informational Blog posts, guides, tutorials, FAQs, explainers "How to", "What is", "Guide", "Tips"
Commercial Comparisons, reviews, "best of" lists, case studies "Best", "Top", "Review", "vs", "Comparison"
Transactional Product pages, service pages, pricing pages, booking forms "Buy", "Order", "Book", "Price", "Sign up"
Navigational Homepage, about page, brand pages, contact pages Brand names, specific website names

Step 4: Optimise Your Existing Content

If you already have content that doesn't match search intent, you have two options:

  • Rewrite the content to match the correct intent. For example, if you have a blog post targeting a commercial keyword, rewrite it as a comparison or review article.
  • Create new content that matches the intent and redirect the old content to the new page (or mark it as noindex).
  • Add supplementary content to the existing page to better serve the intent. For example, add a comparison table to a page that currently only describes one product.

Step 5: Use the Correct Page Title and Meta Description

Your title tag and meta description should clearly communicate what the page is about and match the searcher's intent. This improves your click-through rate (CTR) and helps Google understand your page.

Example – Before & After:

❌ Before (Intent Mismatch):

Title: "Accounting Services in Nairobi – Affordable & Reliable"
Meta: "We offer professional accounting services for SMEs in Nairobi. Contact us today."

Targeting keyword: "best accounting software"

✅ After (Intent Aligned):

Title: "Best Accounting Software for Kenyan Businesses 2026 – Top 10 Reviewed"
Meta: "Discover the best accounting software for Kenyan SMEs. Compare features, pricing, and user reviews. Find the perfect fit for your business."

Targeting keyword: "best accounting software"

Step 6: Create Topic Clusters for Better Intent Coverage

To fully capture all aspects of search intent for your niche, create topic clusters — a pillar page (comprehensive guide) that links to multiple cluster pages (specific subtopics).

Example for a Kenyan SEO agency:

  • Pillar Page: "Complete Guide to SEO in Kenya 2026" (covers everything)
  • Cluster 1: "How to Start SEO for Your Kenyan Business" (Informational)
  • Cluster 2: "Best SEO Agencies in Nairobi – 2026 Review" (Commercial)
  • Cluster 3: "Buy SEO Services in Kenya – Packages & Pricing" (Transactional)

This approach ensures you capture users at every stage of their journey — from learning (informational) to researching (commercial) to buying (transactional).

Keyword Stuffing Without Context

🚨 The Mistake: Forcing keywords into your content in an unnatural, repetitive way

🔤 What Is Keyword Stuffing?

Keyword stuffing is the practice of overloading your content with specific keywords in an attempt to manipulate search engine rankings. It's one of the oldest and most persistent SEO mistakes — and despite Google's many algorithm updates, it's still happening on websites across Kenya and the world.

The problem is that keyword stuffing doesn't work anymore. In fact, it actively hurts your rankings. Google's algorithms are sophisticated enough to detect unnatural keyword usage, and they penalise sites that try to game the system.

What keyword stuffing looks like:

❌ Bad Example – Keyword Stuffing:

"We are the best SEO agency in Nairobi. Our SEO agency in Nairobi offers the best SEO services in Nairobi. If you need SEO in Nairobi, choose our Nairobi SEO agency. Our Nairobi SEO experts are the best SEO experts in Nairobi."

⚠️ This is unreadable, unnatural, and will be penalised by Google.

✅ Good Example – Natural Usage:

"DK Digital is a leading SEO agency in Nairobi. We help businesses across Kenya rank higher on Google through proven search engine optimisation strategies. Whether you're a local startup or an established company, our team can help you attract more organic traffic and grow your online presence."

✅ This reads naturally, uses keywords in context, and provides value to the reader.

⚠️ Why Keyword Stuffing Hurts Your Rankings

Keyword stuffing doesn't just make your content unreadable — it sends negative signals to Google. Here's what happens when you stuff keywords:

  • Poor user experience. Content that reads like a robot wrote it is frustrating to read. Users will leave your page quickly, increasing your bounce rate.
  • Google's algorithm detects unnatural patterns. Google's SpamBrain algorithm is trained to identify keyword stuffing and other manipulative SEO tactics. Sites that trigger SpamBrain can be penalised or even deindexed.
  • Lower quality score. Google's algorithms assess content quality based on factors like readability, natural language, and user engagement. Keyword stuffing scores poorly on all of these.
  • Reduced trust and authority. Users who encounter keyword‑stuffed content perceive the website as spammy or unprofessional. This damages your brand reputation and reduces the likelihood of conversions.
  • It doesn't work anymore. Google's algorithms have evolved to understand context and semantics. They prioritise comprehensive, useful content over keyword‑dense pages.

📊 The Data:

Websites that use natural language and context rank 2.5 times higher on average than those that rely on keyword stuffing (Source: Semrush, 2025). Google explicitly states in its quality guidelines that "keyword stuffing is a violation of our spam policies" and can result in a manual action.

📈 How Keyword Usage Has Changed (2010 vs 2026)

Keyword usage in SEO has evolved dramatically over the last decade. Here's how it's changed:

Aspect 2010 Approach 2026 Approach
Keyword Density Targeting 3‑5% density No fixed density — write naturally
Keyword Placement Force keywords everywhere Use keywords where they make sense

The key takeaway: Keyword stuffing is a relic of the past. Google's algorithms are now so advanced that they can understand the meaning and context of your content — not just the keywords it contains.

🧠 What Is Semantic Search & LSI?

Semantic search is Google's approach to understanding the meaning behind a search query, not just matching keywords. It uses natural language processing (NLP) to interpret context and deliver the most relevant results.

LSI keywords (Latent Semantic Indexing) are words and phrases that are thematically related to your main keyword. They help Google understand the context and depth of your content.

Example — keyword "coffee shop Nairobi":

  • Keyword stuffing approach: Repeat "coffee shop Nairobi" 20 times
  • Semantic/LSI approach: Include related terms like "café", "espresso", "barista", "coffee beans", "breakfast menu", "Wi‑Fi", "outdoor seating", "Kenyan coffee", "latte", "cappuccino", "tea", "pastries"

The semantic approach creates richer, more informative content that helps users and signals relevance to Google. It also ranks for more keywords because your content covers related topics naturally.

🔧 How to Fix Keyword Stuffing – A Step‑by‑Step Guide

Fixing keyword stuffing is about changing your approach to writing content. Follow these steps to create content that's optimised for search engines and readable for humans:

Step 1: Identify Existing Keyword Stuffing Issues

Start by auditing your existing content for keyword stuffing. Look for pages where you have excessively repeated the same keyword or phrase.

  • Use a tool like Yoast SEO or RankMath. These plugins highlight keyword density and warn you if it's too high.
  • Read your content out loud. If it sounds forced or awkward, it probably needs fixing.
  • Check for "keyword cannibalisation". This is when multiple pages on your site target the same keyword, competing against each other.

Step 2: Rewrite for Natural Language

Once you've identified stuffed content, rewrite it to read naturally. The goal is to sound like a human having a conversation with another human.

Before & After Example:

❌ Before (Keyword Stuffed):

"Our Nairobi SEO company offers affordable SEO in Nairobi. As a leading Nairobi SEO agency, we provide Nairobi SEO services that help businesses in Nairobi rank higher. Choose our Nairobi SEO experts for the best Nairobi SEO results."

Keyword: "Nairobi SEO" (9 times in 3 sentences)

✅ After (Natural & Readable):

"DK Digital is a trusted SEO agency in Nairobi, helping businesses across Kenya improve their online visibility. We use proven strategies to help you rank higher on Google, attract qualified leads, and grow your revenue. Whether you're a startup or an established business, our team can help you achieve your digital marketing goals."

Keyword: "SEO agency in Nairobi" (1 time, naturally placed)

Step 3: Use Synonyms and Related Terms

Instead of repeating the same keyword, use synonyms and related terms to create varied, natural content.

📚 Synonyms & Related Terms for "SEO Agency Nairobi":

SEO company Digital marketing agency Search engine optimisation firm Ranking experts Organic traffic specialists Online visibility consultants

Step 4: Focus on Search Intent, Not Just Keywords

Stop writing for keywords and start writing for users. Every piece of content should answer a question, solve a problem, or fulfil a user's need.

  • What is the user trying to achieve? Use this question to guide your content structure.
  • What questions do users have? Include FAQ sections to answer the most common questions.
  • What information is missing from competing content? Go deeper and provide more value than your competitors.

Step 5: Use Keywords Strategically (Not Excessively)

Where should you place keywords?

  • Title tag — include your primary keyword near the beginning
  • Meta description — include the keyword naturally
  • H1 heading — include the primary keyword
  • First paragraph — include the keyword early, naturally
  • Subheadings (H2, H3) — include variations and related terms
  • Image alt text — describe the image, include relevant keywords
  • URL — include the keyword in a clean URL structure

💡 Pro Tip:

A good rule of thumb: write for humans first, search engines second. If you read your content out loud and it sounds natural, you're on the right track. If it sounds robotic or repetitive, it needs work.

Step 6: Expand Your Content with Supporting Information

Instead of stuffing the same keyword, expand your content with relevant sub‑topics and supporting information. This naturally increases the word count and allows you to use a broader range of keywords.

Example — How to Expand Content on "SEO Agency Nairobi":

  • What is SEO and why is it important? (Informational)
  • How to choose the right SEO agency in Nairobi (Commercial)
  • Case studies: Success stories from Nairobi businesses (Trust/Authority)
  • Common SEO mistakes to avoid (Educational)
  • SEO pricing in Kenya (Transactional)
  • FAQs about SEO in Kenya (User questions)

Chasing High-Volume Keywords You Can't Win

🚨 The Mistake: Targeting generic, high-search-volume keywords that you have no chance of ranking for

🎯 What's Wrong With Chasing High-Volume Keywords?

Many website owners make the mistake of targeting only generic, high-search-volume keywords. They see a keyword with 10,000+ monthly searches and think, "If I rank for this, I'll get massive traffic!" While this sounds logical, it's often a recipe for frustration and wasted effort.

The reality is that high-volume keywords are incredibly competitive. They're targeted by established industry giants with years of domain authority, massive backlink profiles, and dedicated SEO teams. As a small or medium-sized Kenyan business, you're competing against the world — and the odds are stacked against you.

The result? You spend months creating content, building backlinks, and optimising your pages — only to find yourself stuck on page 3, 4, or worse. Your high-volume keyword target is out of reach, and you've wasted valuable time and resources that could have been spent on keywords you can actually win.

📊 The Data:

94% of all Google searches are for keywords with less than 500 monthly searches. The remaining 6% are the high-volume keywords that everyone is fighting for. By targeting long-tail keywords, you're going after the 94% of search traffic that most businesses ignore.

⚠️ Why High-Volume Keywords Are So Hard to Rank For

Here's what you're up against when you target a high-volume keyword:

  • High domain authority competitors. Websites like Wikipedia, Amazon, and major news sites dominate the top 10 for high-volume keywords. These sites have been around for decades and have accumulated massive authority.
  • Strong backlink profiles. The top-ranking pages for high-volume keywords often have thousands of backlinks from authoritative websites. It takes years to build that kind of link profile.
  • Large content budgets. Big brands can afford to create thousands of pages of content, hire expert writers, and invest in advanced SEO tools. As a small business, you can't compete on this scale.
  • Established brand recognition. Google favours brands that users recognise and trust. A new or small business simply doesn't have the same brand authority as a global industry leader.
  • High competition for featured snippets. Google's "position zero" (featured snippet) is highly contested for high-volume keywords. You need exceptional content to even have a chance.

The result: Even with perfect on-page optimisation, you're unlikely to crack the top 10 for a high-volume keyword. You're competing against the establishment, and the odds are against you.

🎣 The Solution: Target Long-Tail Keywords

Instead of chasing high-volume keywords, successful SEOs target long-tail keywords — longer, more specific phrases that have lower search volume but much higher conversion potential.

What is a long-tail keyword? It's a keyword phrase that contains 3 or more words and is highly specific. For example, instead of targeting "SEO" (high-volume), target "affordable SEO services for small businesses in Nairobi" (long-tail).

❌ High-Volume (Hard to Rank For)

  • "SEO" (2.4M searches/month)
  • "Digital marketing" (1.2M searches/month)
  • "Web design" (800K searches/month)
  • "SEO agency" (400K searches/month)
  • "Search engine optimisation" (300K searches/month)
⚠️ Extremely competitive. Page 1 dominated by global brands.

✅ Long-Tail (Winnable)

  • "Affordable SEO services for small businesses in Nairobi" (80 searches/mo)

Weak or Missing Internal Linking

🚨 The Mistake: Not connecting your pages with a logical internal linking structure

🔗 What Is Internal Linking and Why Does It Matter?

Internal linking is the practice of linking from one page on your website to another page on the same website. It's one of the most overlooked yet powerful SEO strategies available to website owners.

When you link from Page A to Page B, you're telling both users and search engines that Page B is important and relevant to the topic of Page A. This helps Google:

  • Discover new pages — Googlebot follows internal links to find and index new content on your site.
  • Understand page relationships — Internal links help Google understand how your content is organised and which pages are most important.
  • Distribute link equity (PageRank) — Links pass authority from one page to another, helping important pages rank higher.
  • Improve crawl efficiency — A logical linking structure helps Googlebot crawl your site more efficiently, using less of your crawl budget.

Despite these benefits, most websites have poor internal linking. Pages are left as "islands" with no connections to other content. This is a missed opportunity that hurts both your SEO and user experience.

📊 The Data:

The average website has 31% orphan pages — pages with zero internal links. Websites that fix their orphan pages see a 40‑60% increase in indexed pages within 8 weeks (Ahrefs, 2025). Pages with 5+ internal links rank 2.5x higher than pages with 0‑1 internal links.

🏝️ The Problem: Orphan Pages & Shallow Linking

Orphan pages are pages on your website that have no internal links pointing to them from any other page on your site. They're "orphaned" because they have no parent pages linking to them.

Why are orphan pages a problem?

  • Google may not find them. While Google can discover orphan pages through your sitemap, they're much harder to find and often delayed in indexing.
  • They receive no link equity. Link equity (PageRank) is passed through internal links. Orphan pages have zero incoming internal links, so they receive no link equity — making it harder to rank.
  • Users can't find them. If a page isn't linked from anywhere on your site, users won't discover it naturally. This limits its visibility and engagement.
  • Wasted crawl budget. Googlebot spends time crawling pages that are difficult to find, wasting the crawl budget that could be spent on more important pages.
  • Fragmented user experience. Users who land on an orphan page have no clear path to other relevant content, increasing bounce rate.

Where do orphan pages come from?

  • Old blog posts that were never linked to from newer content
  • Product pages that aren't linked from category or collection pages
  • Service pages that aren't linked from other relevant service pages
  • New pages that were created but not linked from anywhere
  • Deleted pages that remove internal links to other pages (creating new orphans)

You can't fix orphan pages if you don't know they exist. Here's how to find them:

  3. Click the "Internal" tab

Duplicate or Thin Content

🚨 The Mistake: Publishing low‑quality, thin pages or duplicating content across multiple URLs

📄 What Are Thin and Duplicate Content?

Thin content refers to pages that provide little to no value to users. These pages are typically short, superficial, and lack the depth needed to satisfy a user's search intent. Examples include:

  • Pages with under 300 words of content
  • Product pages with manufacturer descriptions only
  • Pages that add no original value beyond what competitors offer
  • Pages that are automatically generated with little human input

Duplicate content is content that appears on multiple URLs on your site (or across different websites). This can happen intentionally or accidentally. Examples include:

  • Same product description used on multiple product pages
  • www vs non-www versions of the same page
  • HTTP vs HTTPS versions of the same page
  • Print versions of pages
  • Session IDs and tracking parameters creating multiple URLs
  • Syndicated content republished from other sources

📊 The Data:

85% of websites have duplicate content issues. Sites with thin content (<300 words per page) rank 2.5x lower on average than sites with comprehensive content (1,500+ words). Google's Helpful Content Update specifically targets thin, unhelpful content — and 29% of websites saw ranking drops after this update alone.

⚠️ Why Thin and Duplicate Content Hurts Your SEO

Both thin and duplicate content send negative signals to Google and users. Here's why they're problematic:

Problem 1: Thin Content

  • Doesn't satisfy search intent. Users arrive at your page expecting a comprehensive answer. Thin content forces them to bounce back to search results — a behaviour Google tracks and penalises.
  • Poor user engagement. Thin content leads to high bounce rates, low time on page, and minimal social sharing — all negative engagement signals.
  • Algorithm penalties. Google's Helpful Content Update and Core Updates explicitly target thin, low‑value content.
  • Missed ranking opportunities. Thin content ranks for fewer keywords and has less topical authority.
  • Poor E‑E‑A‑T signals. Thin content doesn't demonstrate the Experience, Expertise, Authority, or Trustworthiness that Google increasingly requires.

Problem 2: Duplicate Content

  • Confuses Google. When the same content appears on multiple URLs, Google doesn't know which one to index and rank.
  • Dilutes link equity. Backlinks pointing to different versions of the same content don't consolidate their power, reducing the ranking potential of any single page.
  • Wastes crawl budget. Googlebot wastes time crawling duplicate versions of the same page instead of discovering new content.
  • Can trigger penalties. Extensive duplicate content can result in algorithmic demotions or, in extreme cases, manual penalties.
  • Creates user confusion. Users may end up on the wrong version of a page, leading to a frustrating experience.

Page speed is one of Google's confirmed ranking factors. Slow pages frustrate users and send negative signals to search engines.

Why it matters:

  • User experience: 53% of mobile users abandon a page that takes longer than 3 seconds to load.
  • Ranking factor: Google uses page speed as a ranking signal for both desktop and mobile searches.
  • Conversion impact: A 1-second delay in page load time can reduce conversions by 7%.
  • Crawl budget: Slow pages consume more crawl budget, reducing the number of pages Google can crawl on your site.

How to fix slow page speed:

  • Optimise images: Compress images using tools like TinyPNG or ImageOptim. Convert to WebP format for faster loading.
  • Enable caching: Use browser caching and server‑side caching to reduce load times for returning visitors.
  • Use a CDN: A Content Delivery Network (CDN) distributes your content across multiple servers globally, reducing latency for users in different locations.
  • Minify CSS, JavaScript, and HTML: Remove unnecessary characters, spaces, and comments from your code.
  • Reduce server response time: Upgrade your hosting plan or switch to a faster hosting provider.
  • Lazy load images and videos: Only load media when it's about to appear in the user's viewport.
  • Remove render‑blocking resources: Inline critical CSS and defer non‑critical JavaScript.

  • Fix FID: Minimise JavaScript execution time, reduce main thread work, use browser caching.
  • Fix CLS: Set explicit dimensions for images and videos, avoid inserting content above existing content, use proper font loading strategies.

📱 Technical Issue 3: Poor Mobile Responsiveness

Google uses mobile‑first indexing, meaning it primarily uses the mobile version of your site for ranking and indexing. If your site isn't mobile‑friendly, your rankings will suffer.

Why mobile responsiveness matters in Kenya:

How to fix mobile responsiveness issues:

  • Use responsive design: Your site should automatically adjust to fit any screen size.
  • Optimise tap targets: Buttons should be at least 48px × 48px with adequate spacing.

🕷️ Technical Issue 4: Indexing & Crawlability Issues

If Google can't find and index your pages, they won't rank. Indexing and crawlability are the foundation of all SEO.

Common crawlability and indexing issues:

  • Robots.txt blocking important pages: Accidentally blocking Googlebot from accessing key content.
  • Missing XML sitemap: Google needs a sitemap to find all your pages.
  • Noindex tags on important pages: Accidentally telling Google not to index key content.
  • Broken links (404 errors): Links leading to non‑existent pages.
  • Redirect chains: Multiple redirects that slow down crawling.
  • Orphan pages: Pages with no internal links (covered in Mistake #4).
  • JavaScript rendering issues: Googlebot can't render content that requires JavaScript to display.

How to fix indexing and crawlability issues:

  • Check robots.txt: Use Google Search Console's robots.txt tester to ensure you're not blocking important pages.
  • Submit XML sitemap: Create and submit a sitemap to Google Search Console. Include all important pages.
  • Audit noindex tags: Ensure critical pages don't have noindex tags (unless intentionally excluded).
  • Fix broken links: Use tools like Screaming Frog to identify and fix 404 errors.
  • Simplify redirects: Minimise redirect chains — use direct 301 redirects where possible.
  • Use server‑side rendering (SSR): If you use JavaScript frameworks, ensure Googlebot can render your content.
  • Request indexing: After fixing issues, use Google Search Console's "URL Inspection" tool to request indexing.

🔒 Technical Issue 5: Missing or Improper HTTPS

HTTPS (SSL/TLS) is a ranking signal and a security requirement. Sites without HTTPS are flagged as "Not Secure" by browsers, which harms trust and conversions.

Why HTTPS matters:

  • Ranking factor: Google confirmed HTTPS is a ranking signal.
  • Security: Encrypts data between user and server, protecting sensitive information.
  • Trust: Users trust sites with the padlock icon more than those flagged as "Not Secure".
  • Referrer data: HTTPS preserves referrer data, improving analytics accuracy.
  • Modern web standards: HTTPS is increasingly required for modern web features (geolocation, push notifications, etc.).

How to fix HTTPS issues:

  • Install SSL certificate: Purchase and install an SSL certificate from a trusted provider.
  • Force HTTPS: Use 301 redirects to force all traffic to HTTPS version of your site.
  • Update internal links: Ensure all internal links use HTTPS URLs.
  • Update sitemap and canonical tags: Use HTTPS URLs in your sitemap and canonical tags.
  • Test your SSL: Use SSL Labs to test your SSL configuration and identify issues.

🇰🇪 Real Kenyan Example: How Better Titles Tripled Organic Clicks

Client: Nairobi‑Based Law Firm

❌ The Problem:

  • Titles: Generic "Home", "Services", "About Us" — no keywords
  • Meta descriptions: Missing on most pages — Google was auto‑generating
  • CTR: Only 2.5% for top keywords
  • Organic clicks: 85 clicks/month from 3,200 impressions
  • Lead volume: 2‑3 leads per month

✅ The Fix:

  • Rewrote all titles: Included primary keywords, location, brand, and value propositions
  • Created unique meta descriptions: For all 30+ pages with CTAs, emojis, and keywords
  • Optimised for local search: Added "Nairobi", "Kenya", and neighbourhood keywords
  • Used power words: "Top‑Rated", "Trusted", "Experienced", "Expert"

📈 The Results (within 60 days):

  • CTR: 2.5% → 7.8% (3x increase)
  • Organic clicks: 85 clicks/month → 285 clicks/month (+235%)
  • Impressions: 3,200 → 3,650 (up despite no ranking changes)
  • Leads: 2‑3 leads/month → 12‑15 leads/month (+400%)
  • Bounce rate: 72% → 45% (users stayed longer)

☐ Meta description is under 160 characters

☐ Meta description is unique for every page
